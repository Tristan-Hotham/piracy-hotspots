Pirate hotspots, then and now

Piracy never goes away. It moves. I mapped {{n|int}} attacks on ships from 1993 to 2020 and followed the hotspots as they shifted: from the South China Sea and Indonesia to the Strait of Malacca, then to the Gulf of Aden and the open Indian Ocean, then to the Gulf of Guinea, and, in 2025, back to the Singapore Strait.

When people picture pirates today they picture Somali skiffs, ladders against the side of a tanker, a ship held for ransom off the Horn of Africa. That was real, and for four years it dominated the world's piracy. But it was one hotspot among several, and it came and went. The Crime at Sea database, built by Benden, Feng, Howell and Dalla Riva from the reports of the International Maritime Bureau and the US National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, gives a position for {{n|int}} attacks and attempted attacks from 1993 to 2020. Put on a map and played year by year, it shows piracy drifting around the world's shipping lanes like weather.

The database stops in 2020. To bring the story up to today I add the International Maritime Bureau's own headline counts for 2021 to September 2026. Those are totals and regional counts from its press releases, not mapped points, and I keep them apart on this page.

    02 · How the hotspots movedFrom the Malacca Strait to the Gulf of Guinea

    Reported attacks rose from {{low_n|int}} in {{low_year|raw}} to a peak of {{peak_n|int}} in {{peak_year|raw}}, climbed back above 400 a year from 2009 to 2011 ({{n_2009_11|int}} in three years), and then fell to {{n_2020|int}} in 2020. The International Maritime Bureau's counts since then sit between {{imb.y2022|int}} and {{imb.y2025|int}} a year. But the total hides the real story, which is where the attacks happened.

    As

    The 1990s: the South China Sea and Indonesia

    In the 1990s piracy was above all an East and Southeast Asian problem. From 1993 to 1999 the South China Sea, the Philippines and East Asian waters saw {{scs.n_93_99|int}} attacks, {{scs.pct_93_99|pc}} of the world's, more than any other region, with ships boarded off Hong Kong, in the Gulf of Thailand and around the Philippines. Indonesia was close behind and overtook it as the decade ended: attacks there peaked at {{idn.peak_n|int}} in {{idn.peak_year|raw}}, many of them robberies of ships at anchor off Jakarta, Balikpapan, Samarinda and Belawan. Across 1993 to 2004 Southeast and East Asian waters, from the Malacca and Singapore straits to the South China Sea and Indonesia, held {{sea93.pct_93_04|pc}} of all reported attacks.

    The Strait of Malacca falls

    The Strait of Malacca, the narrow lane between Sumatra and Malaysia that carries much of the trade between Europe, the Gulf and East Asia, peaked at {{mal.peak_n|int}} attacks in {{mal.peak_year|raw}}. From 2000 to 2004 it averaged {{mal.avg_00_04|int}} a year. Then it fell: {{mal.n_2005|int}} in 2005, and an average of {{mal.avg_06_20|int}} a year from 2006 to 2020, with no year above {{mal.max_06_20|int}}. The fall came with coordinated sea patrols by Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia from 2004 and joint air patrols from 2005, and after the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami devastated the Aceh coast.

    Somalia: the surge and the collapse

    Then the hotspot jumped west. Attacks in Somali waters, the Gulf of Aden, the southern Red Sea and the Arabian Sea went from {{som.n_2007|int}} in 2007 to {{som.peak_n|int}} in {{som.peak_year|raw}}. Over 2008 to 2011 they were {{som.pct_world_08_11|pc}} of all attacks in the world, and {{som.hij_08_11|int}} of the {{som.hij_world_08_11|int}} hijackings ({{som.hij_pct_world_08_11|pc}}). These were different attacks from the anchorage thefts of Asia: {{som.steam_pct_08_11|pc}} were on ships under way, often far out to sea, and most failed: {{som.att_pct_08_11|pc}} were attempts that did not get on board. Then they collapsed, by {{som.drop_pc_11_13|pc}} between 2011 and 2013, as naval patrols, armed guards, protective measures on ships and action against pirate groups ashore took effect. From 2014 to 2020 the database records only {{som.hij_14_20|int}} hijackings in these waters, all in 2017.

    The Gulf of Guinea rises

    West Africa was never quiet: the anchorages off Lagos are the fourth busiest square in the whole record. But as Somali attacks vanished, the Gulf of Guinea became the world's leading hotspot. It peaked at {{gog.peak_n|int}} attacks in {{gog.peak_year|raw}} and still had {{gog.n_2020|int}} in 2020, {{gog.pct_2020|pc}} of the world's total that year, up from {{gog.pct_93_99|pc}} in the 1990s. The attacks moved further out: the median distance from shore rose from {{gog.kmed_93_15|f1}} km before 2016 to {{gog.kmed_16_20|f1}} km in 2016 to 2020, as gangs from the Niger Delta took crews off ships far offshore and held them for ransom: {{gog.kidnap_desc_16_20|int}} of the {{gog.n_16_20|int}} reports from 2016 to 2020 mention kidnapping. {{gog.fired_16_20|int}} of the {{gog.fired_world_16_20|int}} ships fired upon from 2016 to 2020 were there.

    The Singapore Strait comes back

    The Singapore Strait, the busy channel between Singapore and Indonesia's Batam and Bintan islands, had few attacks before 2008 (about {{sin.avg_93_08|int}} a year). It surged to {{sin.peak_n|int}} in {{sin.peak_year|raw}}; {{sin.boarded_pct_14_15|pc}} of the attacks in 2014 and 2015 were boardings rather than attempts or hijackings. It then dropped to {{sin.n_2016|int}} in 2016. In 2025 the IMB counted {{imb.sin_2025|int}} incidents there, about as many as the 2015 peak and {{imb.sin_pct_2025|pc}} of the world's reported incidents (see section 06).

    03 · What kind of attackMostly theft, sometimes hijacking

    Most piracy is not dramatic. {{types.0.pct|pc}} of all the attacks were boardings, usually by a few robbers who climb aboard, take whatever they can carry, and leave. {{types.3.pct|pc}} were attempts fought off or abandoned. Hijackings, where the attackers take control of the ship, were {{types.1.pct|pc}} ({{hij_total|int}}), peaking at {{hij_peak_n|int}} in {{hij_peak_year|raw}}. Of the attacks where the ship's state is known, {{anchored_berthed_pct_known|pc}} were on ships at anchor or alongside a berth.

    04 · How far from shoreFar out at sea, or close in at anchor

    Half of all attacks happened within {{km_median|f1}} km of land, and {{within_20_pct|pc}} within 20 km. Ships at anchor were attacked a median {{km_anch_median|f1}} km from shore. The Somali attacks broke that pattern. With motherships towing skiffs, Somali pirates hunted ships hundreds of kilometres out: the median distance of attacks in these waters was {{som.median_km_08_11|int}} km in 2008 to 2011, reaching {{som_km_peak|int}} km in {{som_km_peak_year|raw}}, and {{som.far_pct_08_11|pc}} were more than 100 nautical miles (185 km) from land. Of all the attacks that far out, {{far_som_pct|pc}} were in Somali waters.

    05 · Busy lanesWhere the ships are

    Pirates go where the ships are, and where ships slow down or wait. Using the World Bank and IMF map of ship traffic from satellite and coastal AIS signals, summed to 1 degree squares, {{ship.top25_pct_attacks|pc}} of attacks were in the busiest quarter of squares with any traffic, and {{ship.top10_pct_attacks|pc}} in the busiest tenth. But traffic alone does not make a hotspot. The very busiest 1% of squares, in the North Sea, the English Channel and the Baltic approaches, saw only {{ship.top1_pct_attacks|pc1}} of attacks.     The country indicators that come with the database tell a familiar story, with all the usual caution. {{ind_ctx.below_median_gdp_pct|pc}} of attacks inside a country's waters were in countries whose income per person was below the world median that year, and {{ind_ctx.cpi_below35_pct|pc}} in countries scoring under 35 out of 100 on the Corruption Perceptions Index. That is a correlation, not a cause: poorer countries also have many of the world's busiest anchorages and straits.

    06 · TodayWhere piracy happens now

    The mapped data end in 2020. For 2021 to 2026 I use the International Maritime Bureau's published headline counts. They are counts of reported incidents, not positions, and they are not directly comparable with the mapped series, though the database itself was built largely from IMB reports.

    Singapore Strait

    {{imb.sin_2025|int}}incidents in 2025, up from {{imb.sin_2024|int}} in 2024 and {{imb.sin_pct_2025|pc}} of the world's {{imb.y2025|int}}. Falling back in 2026: {{imb.sin_q3_2026|int}} in January to September, against {{imb.sin_q3_2025|int}} a year earlier.

    Somalia

    {{imb.hij_q3_2026|int}}hijackings worldwide in January to September 2026, all by Somali pirates, who took {{imb.som_hostage_pct_2026|int}}% of all crew held hostage. In the mapped data there were {{som.hij_14_20|int}} Somali hijackings from 2014 to 2020.

    Gulf of Guinea

    {{imb.gog_2025|int}}incidents in 2025 ({{imb.gog_2024|int}} in 2024), far below the {{gog.peak_n|int}} mapped in {{gog.peak_year|raw}}, and {{imb.gog_q3_2026|int}} in January to September 2026. Kidnappings of crew still happen: {{imb.kidnap_2025|int}} crew were kidnapped worldwide in 2025.

    Worldwide, the IMB counted {{imb.y2021|int}} incidents in 2021 and {{imb.y2022|int}} in 2022, the lowest for three decades, then {{imb.y2023|int}}, {{imb.y2024|int}} and {{imb.y2025|int}}. In January to September 2026 it counted {{imb.q3_2026|int}}, {{imb.q3_drop_pc|pc}} fewer than the {{imb.q3_2025|int}} of the same months in 2025, mostly because the Singapore Strait went quiet. Indonesia had {{imb.idn_2025|int}} incidents in 2025 and {{imb.idn_q3_2026|int}} in the first nine months of 2026, and the Philippines {{imb.phl_q3_2026|int}} boardings. So in 2026 the picture is two hotspots of very different kinds: petty theft in the busiest strait in the world, and the return of ransom hijacking off Somalia.

    07 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    The attacks. The Crime at Sea database (Benden, Feng, Howell and Dalla Riva, 2021) gives {{n|int}} attacks from 1993 to 2020 with a date, position, attack type, the ship's state (anchored, berthed or steaming), the nearest country and its exclusive economic zone, and the distance to shore. Attacks from 1993 to 2014 ({{src_mapping|int}}) come from the MAPPING piracy data compiled by Brandon Prins from IMB and NGA ASAM reports; those from 2015 to 2020 ({{src_imb|int}}) come straight from IMB reports and include their text. I merged "Boarding" and "Boarded", which the source uses in alternate years for the same thing, and kept the positions as given.

    Regions are simple geography: the Singapore Strait is a box from 103.4°E to 104.65°E and 0.85°N to 1.6°N (including the Batam and Bintan anchorages); the Strait of Malacca a polygon from the Andaman Sea to Singapore; then Indonesian waters, the South China Sea and East Asia, the Indian subcontinent (within 300 km of its coast), the East African coast (within 60 km of Kenya to South Africa), Somali waters (everything else between 32°E and 80°E, 15°S and 30°N, outside the Persian Gulf), West Africa (30°W to 16°E, 20°S to 20°N) and the Americas. Hotspots are 1 degree squares. Shipping comes from the subsampled overview of the World Bank and IMF density raster, so I only use it summed to 1 degree.

    What this is not. These are reported attacks. Many are never reported, especially thefts from small and local craft and attacks on fishing boats; reporting also changes with insurance, politics and who is watching. The 1993 to 2014 records and the 2015 to 2020 records were coded by different people, so small shifts at 2015, such as "fired upon", are partly coding. The IMB counts for 2021 to 2026 are headline numbers from press releases, not mapped points.
